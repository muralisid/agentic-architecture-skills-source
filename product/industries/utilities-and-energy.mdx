## Monday, 7:55, your control room

The overnight shift hands over in four minutes instead of twenty. The event-context agent opens the stand-up: 3,100 alarms overnight, grouped into nine events, seven closed routine, two flagged for the day shift with the asset history, the last comparable incident, and the relevant procedure already attached. Nobody pulled that context by hand; the agent assembled it while the kettle boiled. Out in the field, the first crew of the day opens a work order that already carries the asset's test history and the one past job on this feeder that went wrong. In planning, the register disagreement between GIS and the asset system that would have surfaced mid-outage was flagged on Friday, with a proposed correction waiting for an engineer's yes. The people in this room decide more and dig less. Every one of these patterns is deployable now.

Here is the whole plan to get there.

## Start with these three use cases

Chosen and ranked for you, from the sector's own evidence. Deploy them in this order.

1. **Asset data reconciliation.** Agents find and explain disagreements between your EAM, GIS, and field-captured data, and propose corrections with the evidence attached. First because it is the only one with a safe write path: a wrong proposal costs a rejected change, and every accepted fix improves the data everything else grounds on.
2. **Field crew assistance.** The right past incident, the applicable procedure, and the asset history, retrieved for the job in front of the crew. Second because the value is immediate, the failure is contained, and success is measurable in truck rolls and job time.
3. **Control-room event context.** An agent watches alarm and event streams and assembles the context an operator cannot hold in working memory, presented as suggestions the operator may ignore. Third because it delivers the largest relief where load is worst, once your data and escalation paths have been proven by the first two.

Not on the list, deliberately: switching order generation. It is the use case every vendor demonstrates, and the one your own sector ranks last in adoption, at 1.08 on a five-point scale. Say no to it politely and repeatedly.

## The plan, step by step

1. **Name an owner for each of the three data corpora**: asset register, procedures, incident history. Done when each has a person, not a team, answerable for freshness.
2. **Stand up retrieval inside the perimeter.** Control-system data typically may not leave it, so serving runs local. Done when the field-assistance agent answers from your procedures with the source shown on every answer.
3. **Ship field crew assistance to two depots.** Measure truck rolls avoided and job time before and after. Done when a month of measurements exists and crews ask for it at the depots you did not pick.
4. **Ship reconciliation with an approval queue.** Every proposed correction carries its evidence; an engineer approves in one click; nothing writes without a yes. Done when correction acceptance rate is stable above two thirds.
5. **Wire the validation loop before the control room sees anything.** Raw model output is never displayed: candidate interpretations are checked against rules or a twin, filtered, and only survivors reach the operator, visually distinct from configured alarms. Done when the reversion drill (agent off, room normal) passes.
6. **Ship event context to one control room during a calm season.** Measure acceptance and override rates per operator. Done when operators grant it standing screen space unprompted.
7. **Only now, expand.** More depots, more rooms, more feeders. Autonomy grows one notch at a time, and only where the intervention data supports it.

## Worked example: storm season, 02:00

A feeder trips during a storm burst. Two hundred alarms land in ninety seconds. The event-context agent groups them into one event, pulls the feeder's asset history, flags that the same recloser misoperated twice this year, retrieves the standing storm procedure, and posts one card to the operator's second screen: suspected cause, affected customers, the two prior incidents, the crew nearest with the right qualification. The operator, who retains every decision, dispatches in ninety seconds instead of eleven minutes. The agent wrote nothing to any control system; it never does. The card, the operator's choice, and the timing are all recorded, so the next storm makes the next card better.

## Your agent roster

| Agent | What it does | Autonomy |
|---|---|---|
| Asset reconciliation agent | Finds cross-system disagreements, proposes corrections with provenance | Executes with approval on every write |
| Field assistance agent | Retrieves incident, procedure, and asset context for the job at hand | Read-only; suggests, never acts |
| Event context agent | Assembles alarm context and candidate interpretations for the operator | Read-only; operator holds all authority |

Every agent runs under its own identity with a named human sponsor, and every suggestion is attributable and logged.

## Buy this, build that

Utilities typically carry Microsoft plus a heavy industrial estate. Take identity and the productivity assistants from that incumbent; they are bundled and the wins are real. Build the retrieval layer over your own historian, register, and procedures, because no vendor ships your data curated. Buy field-service agent capability only after asking the vendor which named utility runs it in production with published outcomes; as of August 2026 the honest answer is none, so pilot on your own terms.

## What stays human

Switching authority, safety decisions, emergency judgment, and every action on the control path. Agent output is never an independent protection layer, never appears as a configured alarm, and nothing here touches a safety-instrumented function. Operators do not supervise less as trust grows; the evidence says good operators grant more standing permission and intervene more often, and that is the pattern working, not failing.

## The economics

Count value in the units your business already manages: truck rolls avoided, alarm load per operator during bursts, specialist hours recovered, deferred asset replacement from a cleaner register. The published anchor: model-conversion work that took regional staff weeks was done in under a minute, with the specialist still checking the result. Token costs are noise against a single avoided truck roll.

## Watchouts

- No published production AI alarm-triage deployment exists in an electric utility control room yet. You would be early, which is why the validation loop and the reversion drill are steps, not suggestions.
- No published agent holds autonomous control authority at any named water utility, anywhere. Treat any claim otherwise as a question for the vendor's reference customer.
- Roughly 95% of utility data sits behind cybersecurity and privacy walls. Plan data access before platform choice, not after.
- 30% of sector entities report active bans on public AI tools. Your rollout starts with an approved-tools decision, or it starts as shadow IT.
