<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-01">

This chapter is about teaching a model your field of work. It learns from text your organisation already holds. Think of contracts, support tickets and manuals.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-intro">

This chapter is one of five ways to change the model itself. You pick it when a model does not understand the language of your field.

First, we see how a model learns from text. Then we compare that with training on written examples.

Next, we look at getting the text ready. Then we see how to share out the training effort. Last, we see what changes once the work is done.

</Slide>

## What the model learns from text

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-03">

First, how does a model learn from text at all? It is shown some text with the next word hidden.

It guesses the hidden word. Then it checks the real one. It does this again and again, over huge amounts of text. This is called pretraining.

Every next word is a small test with a known answer. So nobody has to write the answers by hand.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-20">

Most models you use have already learned this way. They learned from text on every kind of subject. Now take one of those models. Keep training it the same way, on your own text. This is called continued pretraining.

The model keeps guessing next words. But now the words come from your field. It comes out reading your field the way your people do.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-04">

Why does guessing words teach the model anything? To guess the next word well, the model must know what the words mean. It must also know what tends to come next in that kind of document.

The model does this over billions of small pieces of text. That forces it to learn the ideas of your field and how people write about them.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-21">

There is also a simple reason models misread specialist text. It comes from how a model reads. A model does not read whole words. It reads text in small pieces. A piece is often just part of a word.

These pieces are called tokens. The rules for cutting text into tokens were learned from everyday text.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-05">

Now take a word that only your field uses. The cutting rules have rarely seen it.

So the word gets cut into four or five pieces that mean nothing. The model has to put the word back together before it can use it.

Continued pretraining teaches the model how these words behave. It learns to read them well in their cut-up form.

</Slide>

## Two kinds of training

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-02">

So far, we have seen how the model learns from text. People often confuse this with another kind of training. In that kind, people write examples by hand. Each example is a question and the answer they want. This is called fine-tuning.

Both kinds train the model further. But they do very different jobs.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-06">

Here they are side by side. Fine-tuning learns from thousands of written question and answer pairs. Continued pretraining learns from text just as it already exists. Text used just as it exists is called raw text. It needs billions of tokens of it.

Fine-tuning teaches the model how to behave. Someone has to write every answer. Continued pretraining teaches the model what your field is. Nobody writes anything. But the text must be prepared first.

</Slide>

## Preparing the text is the project

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-22">

We said the text must be prepared first. Here is what that involves. You gather all the text you plan to train on. That whole collection is called the corpus.

The model takes in everything in the corpus. It does not choose. So preparing the corpus is where the project is won or lost.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-07">

Start by taking out repeats. Old archives hold many templates and near-identical copies. Whatever repeats gets too much weight in training.

Then filter out noise. Failed scans, broken files and machine-made logs teach the model noise.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-08">

Some text must never go in at all. Keep out anything you are not allowed to keep. Also keep out anything you would not want the model to say back.

Old support tickets can hold passwords. Those stay out.

Personal data stays out if you have no legal reason to keep it. Private legal advice stays out too.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-16">

Some data is not written text at all. It sits in tables and records. That data stays out of the corpus too.

When the model needs that data, it looks it up. It asks a small program to fetch it. A program like this is called a tool.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-23">

Next comes the question of rights. Before training, check that you are allowed to use the text this way. Being able to read the text does not always mean you may train on it.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-09">

Rules also follow the text into the model. A model trained on your corpus is made from that corpus. So the rules for that text now apply to the model too.

Some rules say how sensitive the text is. Some say where it may be kept. Some say when it must be deleted.

And a model cannot forget just one thing. If one record must be deleted, the model cannot drop just that record.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-10">

One more thing goes into the corpus. If you train only on your own text, the model gets better at your field. But it can get worse at everything else.

It loses some of the general skills it had before. This is called forgetting.

So your text is mixed with general text. General text is everyday writing on every kind of subject. It helps the model keep its general skills.

</Slide>

## How much goes where

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-11">

With the corpus ready, the next question is how to share out the training. Say you can afford a fixed amount of training. You must decide how much goes to continued pretraining and how much to fine-tuning.

One study tested exactly this. It looked at health, chemistry and coding. In each one, the best split put almost everything into continued pretraining.

It is one study at one model size. Use it as a direction. Do not copy the exact split.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-12">

So the lesson is about order and size. Learning your field is the big job. It comes first, and it uses raw text.

Learning the task is the small job on top. It uses question and answer pairs.

Some teams write tens of thousands of pairs by hand to add knowledge. That is the wrong way to do the big job.

</Slide>

## Putting it to work

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-14">

Now let us see what this changes in practice. A general model is one trained only on general text. Here is what it gets wrong in your field.

It reads a common word in its everyday sense. Your field may use that word to mean something else.

It treats your reference codes as noise. It takes your insider slang for a typo.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-15">

Every one of these misses costs something. The terms must be looked up and explained on every request. Looking up text to add to a request is called retrieval.

The system still works. But everything has to be explained each time.

Continued pretraining puts that knowledge into the model itself. Then the terms no longer need explaining each time.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-17">

Here is the order of work. First, continued pretraining on the corpus teaches your field. If the model was fine-tuned before, run that fine-tuning again on top. This brings back the style and format of its answers.

Next, test the model's general skills. If they are damaged, repair them. Use the fix for forgetting from the distillation chapter.

Your system may send each request to a model of the right size. This is called routing. Last, check the routing again. A small model that knows your field can now take more routine requests.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-18">

So what changes afterwards? Retrieval stops spending its space on definitions. It brings back evidence for the task instead.

The text sent to the model with each request is called the prompt. Prompts get shorter. A list of definitions no longer needs pasting in.

Each request spends fewer tokens on explaining terms. So each request costs less. The model also makes better use of what retrieval finds.

</Slide>

## The test for moving on

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-19">

So when should you move past this step? There is rarely anywhere further to go. Continued pretraining plus fine-tuning is the end of the road for almost every organisation.

The last chapter is [custom pretraining and architecture](/ladder/custom-pretraining). It is only for data that is not text at all.

</Slide>

## What to remember

<Slide journey="ladder/continued-pretraining" id="cp-recap">

Here is what to remember. A model learns your field by guessing the next word in your own text. Preparing that text is where the project is won or lost.

Mix in general text to keep the model's general skills. Learn the field first, from raw text. Then learn the task on top, from pairs.

For almost everyone, this is as far as the ladder needs to go. The next and last chapter is custom pretraining and architecture.

</Slide>
