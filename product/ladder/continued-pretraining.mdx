<PlainTerms>
This is how you teach a model your world: not from labelled examples but from raw text, the material your organisation already has in bulk. The model keeps doing what pretraining always did, predicting the next word, but now over your contracts, filings, tickets and manuals, and it comes out reading your domain the way your people do. It is the option that suits an organisation with a large corpus, it is routinely confused with fine-tuning, and the corpus preparation is where the project is won or lost.
</PlainTerms>

## What the model actually learns from raw text

Next-token prediction sounds too simple to teach anything. It is worth seeing why it is not.

To predict the next word in "the beneficial owner of the account is", a model must have absorbed what beneficial ownership is, what kinds of entities own accounts, and what tends to follow in the kind of document this is. Prediction quality is a proxy for understanding, and improving it over billions of tokens of your material forces the model to absorb your domain's concepts, relationships and conventions. Nobody labels anything, because the text itself is the supervision: every next word is a tiny test with a known answer.

There is also a mechanical reason general models read specialist text badly, and it is visible before any learning happens. Models read in <Term k="token">tokens</Term>, and a tokeniser trained on general text splits unfamiliar vocabulary into fragments: a drug name, a legal term of art, or a SWIFT message type becomes four or five meaningless shards. The model spends capacity reassembling words your industry treats as atoms. Continued pretraining teaches the model your vocabulary's behaviour even through awkward tokenisation, which is part of why fluency in the domain improves so visibly.

## The distinction, one more time

| | Supervised <Term k="fine-tuning">fine-tuning</Term> | Continued pretraining |
|---|---|---|
| Input | Pairs: a question and the wanted answer | Raw text, as it already exists |
| Volume | Thousands of examples | Billions of tokens |
| Teaches | How to behave | What the domain is |
| Preparation | High: someone writes the answers | Different: nobody writes anything, but the corpus must be curated |

The preparation row deserves expansion, because "you already have the data" is true and misleading.

## The corpus work is the project

A model absorbs whatever the corpus contains, indiscriminately. The work is deciding what that should be:

- **Deduplication.** Enterprise archives repeat: templates, boilerplate, forwarded chains, near-identical versions. Repetition over-weights whatever repeats.
- **Quality filtering.** OCR failures, corrupted exports and machine-generated logs teach noise.
- **Contamination.** Anything you must not retain, and anything you would not want generated back: credentials in old tickets, personal data outside its retention basis, privileged material.
- **The rights question.** Training on it requires the right to, and obligations follow the weights. A model trained on your corpus is a derived artifact of it, so classification, residency and erasure obligations reach the resulting model. [The concern matrix](/architecture/concern-matrix) sets out that inheritance, and it is sharper here than anywhere else on this ladder, because the artifact is large, expensive, and impossible to selectively forget.
- **The mix.** Training only on domain text sharpens the domain and dulls everything else. Practice mixes domain material with general text to hold general capability, and the forgetting repair on [distillation](/ladder/distillation) applies here as it did to fine-tuning.

## How much goes where: the budget split

<TeachingIllustration src="/images/ladder/continued-pretraining-corpus-labeled-v2.webp" alt="A disordered archive passes through rights, duplication, contamination, quality and balance filters while a sealed evaluation collection stays outside training." title="The corpus is the product" caption="Rights, provenance, deduplication, contamination control, quality and balance determine what the model learns. The evaluation set must remain sealed outside that production line or the final score stops meaning anything." />

One study modelled the allocation directly: a fixed 30-billion-token budget split between continued pretraining and supervised fine-tuning, 7B-parameter models, across health, chemistry and coding. The optimum in every domain put **roughly 99.99 percent of the budget into continued pretraining** and the small remainder into fine-tuning, and reported an 11.6 percent improvement over a state-of-the-art domain-adapted comparison at up to 95 percent less budget (OpenReview, 8 October 2025).

Read it as a direction, not a recipe: one study, three domains, one model size. The durable conclusions are the ordering and the proportion. Domain adaptation is the big job and is done on raw text; task behaviour is the small job on top and is done on pairs. Teams that invert this, hand-writing tens of thousands of pairs to inject knowledge, are doing the expensive job with the wrong instrument.

## At the bank

The bank holds decades of exactly the material this step wants: filed suspicious activity reports and their narratives, case files, typology guidance, internal policy, regulator correspondence, procedure manuals. Hundreds of millions to billions of tokens, written in the dialect the agent is supposed to speak.

**What the base model gets wrong, concretely.** It reads "structuring" as organisation rather than as deposits split to evade thresholds. It parses SWIFT field references as noise. It treats "smurfing" as a typo. Every such miss is context the retrieval layer must carry on every call: the agent works, but it works like a bright temp on their first week, needing everything explained. Continued pretraining is how the temp becomes staff.

**What goes in the corpus, and what does not.** In: the filed reports, guidance, policies, manuals, anonymised case narratives. Out: raw transaction data, which is structured data the model reaches through tools, not prose to absorb; anything under erasure obligations, because weights cannot forget a customer; and correspondence containing personal data outside its retention basis. The corpus review is run jointly with the privacy office, and that is not overhead, it is the project.

**The sequence.** Continued pretraining on the corpus teaches the dialect. The chapter 4 fine-tune re-runs on top to restore the house narrative shape. The general-capability eval runs, and the distillation repair follows if it shows damage. Then the routing economics from chapter 5 are recomputed, because a domain-adapted small model handles a wider routine tier than a general one did.

**What changes downstream.** Retrieval budget stops being spent on definitions and starts being spent on evidence. Prompts shrink because the model no longer needs the typology glossary inline. The measured effect to expect is not "smarter" but cheaper and sharper: fewer tokens of explanation per alert, better use of what is retrieved.

## The test for moving on

There is rarely anywhere further to go. Continued pretraining plus fine-tuning is the end of the road for almost every enterprise. The final chapter, [custom pretraining and architecture](/ladder/custom-pretraining), exists for one specific situation: data that is not text at all.
