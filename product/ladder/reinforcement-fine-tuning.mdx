<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-01">

Most training shows a model examples of right answers. Training a model further on examples like these is called fine-tuning. But good judgement is hard to show with examples. There is a kind of fine-tuning that can teach it.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-intro">

The ladder is the order in which to try changes to an agent. The early steps change the system around the model. The later steps change the model itself. Reinforcement fine-tuning is one of those later steps.

First, we look at how training with scores works.

Then we see how the score can go wrong.

Next comes the warning sign to watch for.

Then we see what this training can fix and what it cannot.

Last, we look at where to go next.

</Slide>

## How training against a grader works

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-20">

First, how this training works. Ordinary fine-tuning pulls a model towards fixed right answers. This kind works differently. The model tries the task in its own way. Each attempt gets a score. The model is pulled towards whatever scores well. This is called reinforcement fine-tuning.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-02">

Here is the loop, step by step. First, the model makes an attempt at the task.

Next, something checks the attempt and gives it a score. This checker is called a grader.

Then training makes the behaviour behind high scores more likely. The loop runs again and again.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-03">

This freedom to search is where the power comes from. On some tasks, good answers vary a lot. You cannot list them all as examples. On tasks like these, the model can find ways of working that no example showed.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-21">

This training can also work with few tasks. The company that sells it reported gains from about a hundred graded tasks. Ordinary fine-tuning on that few examples made the model worse. A result a seller reports about its own product is called a vendor result. So this one is flagged as a vendor result.

</Slide>

## What makes a grader gameable

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-04">

The same freedom to search also brings a danger. The model chases the score. It does not know what you really meant.

Say the score and your real goal differ somewhere. The model will find that gap. It gets far more attempts than you spent building the grader.

This is not cheating on purpose. Shortcuts that score well simply become more likely. Getting a high score without doing the real job is called gaming the grader.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-05">

Here are four common ways a grader gets gamed. The first is surface style. Say tidy, confident answers score well. Then the model learns to look tidy and confident, right or wrong.

The second is length. If long answers score well, expect the model to pad them out.

The third is using another AI model to give the score. A model used this way is called a judge. The judge has its own biases. It tends to favour some kinds of answer. That becomes part of the score.

The fourth is checks that miss things. Say the grader checks three things. The model gets good at those three and neglects the rest.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-06">

Judges have one well-known bias. Related models from the same maker form a group called a model family. A model tends to prefer answers in the style of its own family. We saw this in our own experiments. A judge from the same family found a difference. That difference vanished when a judge from another family checked it.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-12">

Gaps can also hide in old records. It is tempting to score a model against decisions people made in the past. But those decisions include people's own mistakes. Say a decision to act gets checked again later. A decision to do nothing is almost never looked at again.

So a wrong decision to do nothing stays hidden. A grader built on these records would teach the model the same blind spots.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-07">

So when is a grader safe? It is safe when a program can check the result. That program must not share the model's blind spots.

Think of code that must pass its tests. Or a figure that must match its source document. Or a schedule that must meet every rule.

Some quality comes down to taste or tone. No program can check that. There, the grader is only a stand-in. And a stand-in will be gamed.

</Slide>

## The warning sign to watch for

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-08">

So how do you spot a grader that is being gamed? Say the model scores higher than a careful person on the same task. Do not treat that as a win. It means the model learned the scoring rules instead of the job. The score works like a reward for the model. So this failure is called reward hacking. When it happens, treat the grader as the broken part.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-09">

So look after the grader like any important software. Keep track of every change to it. Have someone review each change. Test it. And give it a named owner. A fault in the grader trains the same fault into the model. That model gives no warning. It just scores very well.

</Slide>

## What it fixes, and what it does not

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-17">

Now, what can this training fix? It fixes judgement on tasks where a program can check the result. It does not fix format, tone or style. Those are easy to show with examples. Ordinary fine-tuning handles them.

It also fixes reasoning over many steps. But it cannot add knowledge the model does not have. It changes how the model behaves. It does not store new facts.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-10">

There are published gains from this training in specialist fields. They come from the company that sells it. They describe its own customers. So they are vendor results.

They cover tax analysis and a medical task scored against doctors. They also cover pulling references out of documents.

</Slide>

## The test

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-18">

The last part is where to go next. Say the model's judgement is now right. But it still misreads the material in your field.

Then what is missing is knowledge of your field. You can add it by training the model further on plain text from your field. This is called [continued pretraining](/ladder/continued-pretraining). And once quality is proven, a smaller model can learn to do the job more cheaply.

</Slide>

## What to remember

<Slide journey="ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning" id="rf-recap">

Here is what to remember. Before you start, you need a grader that only the real job can satisfy.

This training moves the model towards whatever scores well.

So any gap between the score and your goal will be found.

If the model beats a careful person, check the grader first.

This training fixes judgement. It does not add knowledge. Next, we look at continued pretraining.

</Slide>
