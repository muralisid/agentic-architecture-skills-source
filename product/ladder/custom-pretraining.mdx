<PlainTerms>
At the top of the market two very different things get sold in similar language. One is building your own foundation model from scratch, which is a question of economics and almost never the answer. The other is designing a different kind of model because your data is not documents at all but networks, sequences or signals, which is a real discipline with a recognisable use. This page explains both in enough depth that you can tell which one is in front of you, and whether either applies. The one thing to remember: everything below this chapter works around a fixed model; this chapter is about building models, which is a different profession.
</PlainTerms>

## Path one: a foundation model of your own

**What it is.** Pretraining from scratch: initialise weights randomly, train on trillions of tokens, then align the result into something usable. Everything the base model knows must come from your assembled corpus, because there is no base model.

**What it costs.** Frontier-laboratory economics: compute in the tens of millions and up, a standing research team, and the time between deciding and having something competitive, during which the field moves. And the artifact is perishable: the open and commercial models improve on someone else's budget, while yours improves on yours.

**When it is genuine.** Sovereignty mandates that forbid external weights outright, at national or systemically-regulated scale. Or a corpus so large and so unlike public text that general models are structurally wrong for it, together with the money and the team. If you are reading this page to find out whether you qualify, you do not, and that is most of what the page is for.

**The question that deflates most proposals.** What would [continued pretraining](/ladder/continued-pretraining) on the same corpus achieve? It delivers most of what people actually want from "our own model", from the same data, at a small fraction of the cost, on an upgrade path someone else maintains.

**One published result, with its flags.** If you are on this path, when the domain data enters matters: mixing 0.1 to 10 percent of domain data into pretraining from scratch outperformed the standard general-pretrain-then-adapt pipeline across three domains, cutting the tokens needed for a given domain performance by up to 1.75 times [vendor]. Scope: models of 300M to 3B parameters, a 200B-token budget, published by a company selling data curation. It informs how to run path one, not whether to take it.

## Path two: when your data is not text

A language model is a machine for sequences of tokens. Some enterprise data is not that, and no amount of prompting, retrieval or fine-tuning makes it that. Transaction networks, supply chains, telemetry streams, molecular structures. For these, the model itself is different, most commonly a graph neural network, and the engineering discipline around it is different too.

**How a graph model works, in one paragraph.** Each node starts with a vector describing it. In each layer, every node updates its vector using its neighbours' vectors, so after one layer a node knows about its neighbours, after two it knows about their neighbours, and so on outward. Prediction then reads the node vectors: is this account likely part of a ring, is this component about to fail. The structure itself, who connects to whom, is what the model learns from, which is exactly what a document model cannot see.

**The failure modes are structural, and they are the vocabulary to listen for.**

- **Over-smoothing.** Each layer averages neighbours. Stack enough layers and every node in a connected region converges to the same vector: depth destroys the distinctions the model exists to draw. This is why production graph models are often only two or three layers deep.
- **Over-squashing.** The opposite pressure. Information from a node several hops away must pass through an exponentially growing neighbourhood compressed into fixed-size vectors, so long-range signal is crushed at bottleneck edges.
- The two **pull against each other**: layers added to relieve over-squashing worsen over-smoothing. This trade is the tell for genuine expertise. Someone who has really worked here has had to resolve it structurally, with rewiring, attention or hierarchical pooling, not with a bigger setting.
- **Representation and gradient collapse.** The general deep-learning failures: learned vectors that stop being distinct, and learning signal that dies through depth, so parts of the network silently stop training.
- **Partitioning and sampling.** A graph with billions of edges does not fit in accelerator memory, and unlike documents it cannot be arbitrarily cut: every cut severs real relationships. Partition into clusters and you train on a graph missing its cross-cluster edges; sample neighbourhoods and you introduce variance and bias in what each node sees. Both change the object being trained on, and managing that gap is where much of the real work lives.
- **Distributed convergence.** Sharded across many machines, optimisation stops behaving like the single-machine textbook: giant effective batches change the loss landscape, stale gradients arrive from busy workers, and communication cost starts dictating the algorithm.

**When it is genuine.** The thing you are predicting is a property of a structure rather than of a document, at a scale where these failure modes actually bite. That situation is recognisable, and it has little to do with agents.

## In utilities and mining

A specialist model could estimate a production quantity, characterize an equipment sequence, or analyze relationships in a network. The agent can use that model through a tool without being the model itself.

Start with the measurement or prediction task and a credible reference outcome. Compare conventional features, existing models, and simpler statistical methods before building a new architecture. Include the data, research, deployment, and maintenance effort.

Keep the specialist’s units, uncertainty, calibration, and applicable conditions with its result. An agent’s fluent explanation must not turn an uncertain estimate into an official production record. See [production measurement](/use-cases/production-measurement).

## How this connects back

Everything below this chapter works **around a fixed model**. This chapter **builds models**. Different budgets, different staff, different risk lifecycle, meeting at a tool boundary.

The instinct, though, transfers all the way down, and it is the instinct this whole ladder teaches: diagnose the structural failure before spending, and never assume a bigger version of what you have will fix it.
