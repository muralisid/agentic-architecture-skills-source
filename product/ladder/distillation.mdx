<PlainTerms>
Everything else on this ladder tries to make an agent better. This one keeps it the same and makes it cheaper. A large model is used to teach a small one, and the small one is what you run. It belongs on the ladder because it is the answer to a real complaint, which is not "this is wrong" but "this is too slow" or "this costs too much". It also has a second use that is easy to miss: it repairs the damage that domain training causes.
</PlainTerms>

## What it changes

Which model runs in production. A capable teacher model produces outputs, and a smaller student model is trained to reproduce them. You deploy the student.

## What it fixes, and what it does not

**Fixes:** unit cost and latency at roughly the same quality, on a bounded task. **Does not fix:** anything about quality. If the teacher is wrong, the student learns to be wrong faster and more cheaply.

## The second use: repairing forgetting

Training a model on your domain degrades its general instruction-following. On-policy distillation repairs this, by distilling from the **pre-fine-tune version of the same model** as teacher, at 9 to 30 times lower training compute than scaling supervised fine-tuning. That makes distillation maintenance rather than only compression, and it is the reason it sits between [adapters](/ladder/adapters-and-fine-tuning) and the heavier options rather than at the end.

## When it is worth it

A narrow, high-volume workload, where the same shape of request runs constantly, and where a locked eval can prove the student holds quality. The tension worth naming: the business case needs a fixed eval set, and a live learning loop keeps rewriting the eval set. Decide which you are running before you commit.

## The test

There is no climb from here. Distillation is a move sideways and down, and it can follow any of the other options in this deck.
