<PlainTerms>
When an agent is not good enough, there are seven different things you can change, and they are not equally sensible to try. Rewriting the instructions is cheap and often enough. Retraining the model is expensive, slow, and usually fixes the wrong problem. This page puts the seven in order, and gives each one a test that tells you whether you have genuinely run out of room before you spend money on the next. The one thing to remember: almost every organisation that believes it needs to train a model has not yet exhausted the three rungs below that.
</PlainTerms>

## Why the order matters

**WHY:** "The model is not good enough" is a conclusion, not a diagnosis. It is reached most often when nobody has measured what is actually failing. The published evidence runs the other way: prompt and context work keeps producing gains that teams assumed were exhausted, and the expensive interventions frequently fix something that was never the problem. Climbing early is not just wasted money. It adds weight, lock-in and a maintenance burden to a system whose real fault is still there underneath.

**WHAT:** Treat improvement as a ladder with seven rungs and a fixed order. Each rung changes something different, costs more than the one below it, and has an **exit test**: a measurable signal that you have taken it as far as it goes. Do not climb without passing the test. Everything rests on an <Term k="eval">evaluation</Term> suite you own, because every exit test is a measurement.

## The seven rungs

**In short:** Each rung changes a different thing. The gate on each is the evidence that justifies the next step.

<GuideFigure id="intelligence-ladder" fullWidth />

| # | Rung | What it changes | Climb when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Instructions | The wording of the task | Accuracy climbs during a week of prompt work, then goes flat against a locked eval |
| 2 | Context | What reaches the model, and how documents are represented | Your representation beats a <Term k="matched control">matched control</Term>, and what still fails is acting rather than knowing |
| 3 | Tools and the loop | The system around the model | The loop is instrumented and traced, and the residue is the model not knowing your domain |
| 4 | Adapters | A small set of extra weights | Adapters close the format and vocabulary gap but leave the judgement gap open |
| 5 | <Term k="distillation">Distillation</Term> | Which model runs, at the same capability | Not a climb. A move down in cost and latency |
| 6 | Reinforcement <Term k="fine-tuning">fine-tuning</Term> | Weights, against a grader | You have a programmable grader that cannot be gamed, and a demonstrated ceiling |
| 7 | Pretraining and re-architecture | The model itself | Essentially never, for an enterprise |

Two things about this list are worth stating plainly, because both are commonly got wrong.

**Rung 3 is the one most often skipped.** Teams go from "the context is good and it is still failing" straight to training. The evidence says otherwise: holding task, environment and model constant, harness choices measurably change the decisions an agent makes even where the final success rate looks the same (arXiv 2607.04528, July 2026), and in retrieval-heavy work the harness can matter more than the retrieval architecture itself (arXiv 2605.15184, May 2026). Most organisations that think they need a trained model need better tools and a better loop.

**Rung 5 is not a climb at all.** Distillation buys the same capability from a cheaper, faster model. You reach for it because of unit cost or latency, never because quality is short. It also has a second use that is easy to miss: on-policy distillation repairs the instruction-following that domain fine-tuning degrades, by distilling from the pre-fine-tune version of the same model. If you have climbed to rung 4 or 6 and something got worse, rung 5 is the published repair.

## The rails: an evaluation suite you own

**In short:** Every exit test above is a measurement, so without an eval suite there is no ladder, only opinion.

An organisation that cannot measure its agent will climb on anecdote, and anecdote favours whichever intervention is most fashionable. Build the suite from real failures rather than imagined ones, before the agent rather than after, and have it owned by the domain experts who can say what a correct answer looks like.

One hazard belongs here rather than further up: when a validation score exceeds what a careful human achieves on the same task, the system has learned the rubric rather than the job. Treat that as an alarm, not a result.

## What is measured here, and what is not

**In short:** The rungs an enterprise actually lives on are the ones we measured. The rungs above rest on published work, and are labelled as such.

Rung 2 carries our own experiments: same corpus, same encoder, changing only how documents are represented moves <Term k="ndcg">nDCG@10</Term> from 0.294 to 0.803, and our own clever representation lost to plain chunking on real prose, which we published. Rungs 4 to 7 need budget and hardware this programme does not have, so they are documented from the literature with their evidence status marked on each page. That division is deliberate, and stated rather than hidden.

<PrincipleCard eyebrow="The rule" title="Climb only on evidence that the rung below is exhausted" kind="evidence">
Each rung costs more than the one beneath it in money, time, skill and lock-in. The exit test is what converts "we think the model is the problem" into a measurement. Organisations that skip the test usually arrive at rung 4 or above carrying a fault that rung 2 or rung 3 would have fixed, and now have a trained artifact to maintain as well.
</PrincipleCard>
