<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-01">

When an AI system is not good enough, there are eight things you can change. They are not equally sensible to try. This video shows the order worth trying them in.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-intro">

Here is what this video covers. We start with two simple words you will need.

Then we look at why the order of changes matters.

Next, three changes to the system around the AI.

Then five changes to the AI itself.

Last, how to measure every change. That is how you know what worked.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-model">

At the heart of any AI system is a program that reads text and writes text back. You give it words, and it answers with words. This program is called the model.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-agent">

A model on its own only writes answers. Often we want more. We want it to finish a whole task.

So we build a system around the model. It lets the model take actions, one step after another. This is called an agent.

</Slide>

## Why the order matters

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-02">

Now you know what a model and an agent are. So why does the order of changes matter?

When an agent fails, a common first reaction is to blame the model. People say the model is not good enough. But that does not say what is actually wrong. It is usually said before anyone has measured what is failing.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-03">

The usual answer is to change the model itself. You feed it new examples, and it learns from them. This is called training.

Training too early costs a lot of money.

It also makes the system heavier. And it can tie you to one model.

You now have a trained model to look after. And the real fault is still sitting there underneath.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-04">

Published work points the other way. Better wording and better information for the model are cheap to try. They keep finding gains that teams thought were used up.

The expensive changes often fix something that was never the problem.

</Slide>

## Deck A: change the system

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-07">

We saw why jumping to training too early is costly. Now, here are all eight things you can change.

The first three change the system around the model. The model itself stays exactly the same.

The last five change the model itself. These are the expensive ones.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-08">

The first three work like a ladder. You climb them in order. Finish each step before the next one is worth trying. We call this group Deck A.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-09">

Step one is the [instructions](/ladder/instructions). You write words that tell the model what the task is. Those words are called the prompt.

Keep improving the prompt. Measure how often the answers are right. Move on when that climbs for a week of work, then goes flat.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-10">

Step two is [context](/ladder/context). Everything that reaches the model along with the task is called context. It includes your documents, and what the agent remembers from before.

First, your way of preparing documents must beat a plain, simple version. Test both on the same cases. That keeps it fair. Then move on when the failures left are about taking action, not missing knowledge.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-loop">

Before step three, one more idea. An agent works in rounds. First, it picks an action.

Then it looks at the result.

Then it decides what to do next, and goes round again. This repeating cycle is called the loop.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-11">

Step three is the [tools and the loop](/ladder/tools-and-the-loop). The agent can do things, like search a file or save a note. Each of these abilities is called a tool.

Record every round of the loop. Then you can trace what happened. Move on when what still fails is the model not knowing your field.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-12">

Of the three steps, step three is the one most often skipped. Teams see that the context is good, but the agent still fails. So they jump straight to training.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-13">

Published work kept the task and the model fixed, and changed only the loop. The agent made different decisions. That happened even when the success rate looked the same.

For tasks that are mostly about finding information, the loop can matter more than how the search is built. Most teams who think they need a trained model need better tools and a better loop.

</Slide>

## Behaviour and knowledge are different jobs

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-finetune">

That was Deck A. Now we turn to changing the model itself.

One way is to show the model thousands of examples. Each example is a task paired with a good answer. The model learns to behave like the examples. This is called fine-tuning.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-pretrain">

There is a second way to teach a model. A model first learns by reading a huge amount of text.

You can let it keep reading, using your own documents. It learns the words and ideas of your field. This is called continued pretraining.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-14">

Now put the two side by side. Teaching a model how to behave and teaching it about your field are different jobs.

Fine-tuning learns from thousands of examples. Continued pretraining learns from raw text. That just means plain documents. It needs billions of small pieces of words.

Fine-tuning changes how the model behaves. Continued pretraining changes what it knows, and how it reads your language.

So fine-tuning is the wrong tool for adding knowledge. And continued pretraining is the wrong tool for fixing format or tone.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-15">

This matters for most companies. A company with lots of its own documents has plenty of raw text. But it often has very few example pairs. That makes continued pretraining the natural option for it.

It also explains why so many fine-tuning projects disappoint. The wrong tool was picked for the problem.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-16">

One more point about both methods. Some facts are about one customer, one case or one policy. Those facts belong in neither method. They stay in documents the agent looks up, and in the agent's [governed memory](/ladder/context#memory-is-context-that-lasts).

There, each fact can be shown with its source, updated or deleted. A trained model cannot do any of those things.

</Slide>

## Deck B: change the model

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-17">

We saw two different ways to teach a model. Now, how to choose among the five changes to the model.

These five do not run in order. You look at what is failing, and pick the change that fits. You may need more than one. We call this group Deck B.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-adapters">

The first symptom is wrong format, tone or task behaviour. The fix is to teach new behaviour with fine-tuning.

You do not have to change the whole model. You can train a small add-on that sits on top of it. This add-on is called an adapter.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-distill">

The second symptom is different. The answers are good, but the model is too slow or too expensive.

Then a large model can teach a smaller, cheaper model to give the same answers. This is called distillation.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-reinforce">

The third symptom is right format, but wrong judgement. The answer looks right, but the decision in it is wrong.

This fix needs results that can be checked and scored. The model tries, gets a score, and does more of what scored well. This is called reinforcement fine-tuning.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-read">

The fourth symptom is about understanding. The model does not understand the words, documents or symbols of your field.

We met this fix earlier. Let the model keep reading your own text with continued pretraining.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-custom">

The last symptom is about the data itself. Sometimes your data is not text at all. It might be a web of connections, long sequences, or signals, in huge amounts.

Then look at a model built specially for that kind of data. This is called a custom architecture.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-18">

Here is the whole map in one place. Wrong format, tone or behaviour points to [adapters and fine-tuning](/ladder/adapters-and-fine-tuning).

Too slow or too costly points to [distillation](/ladder/distillation).

Wrong judgement points to [reinforcement fine-tuning](/ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning).

Misreading your field points to [continued pretraining](/ladder/continued-pretraining).

Data that is not text points to [custom architecture](/ladder/custom-pretraining).

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-19">

These options often combine. A standard pairing is continued pretraining, then fine-tuning. The first teaches the field. The second teaches the task.

Distillation can come after any of the others. It makes the final model smaller and cheaper to run.

</Slide>

## Measure every change

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-20">

That covers all eight changes. Now, the one thing every change depends on.

Every decision to move on is based on a measurement. So you need a fixed set of real cases with known correct answers. You own it, and you test every change against it. This is called an evaluation suite.

Without one, teams pick changes based on stories. And stories favour whatever is most fashionable.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-21">

Here is how to build that suite.

Build it from real failures, not imagined ones.

Build it before the agent, not after.

Have experts in your field own it. They know what a correct answer looks like.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-22">

Some agents keep a memory from one task to the next. If yours does, the suite also tests what memory can break.

Test a fact that changed over time. Test a question the record cannot answer.

Test a record that was deleted. And test a person who is not allowed to see it.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-23">

One warning belongs here. Sometimes a score comes out higher than a careful person gets on the same task.

That means the system learned to score well without doing the job. This is called reward hacking. Treat it as an alarm. Check how the score is given before you trust it.

</Slide>

## What this rests on

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-24">

Before we finish, a note on the evidence. Deck A rests partly on our own measurements.

Deck B comes from published work, and from the companies that make the models.

Splitting the eight changes into two decks is my own view.

</Slide>

## What to remember

<Slide journey="ladder/index" id="ov-recap">

Here is what to remember. First, change the system before you change the model.

Deck A runs in order and costs little. The evidence says most of the gain is still there.

Deck B is picked by what is failing. It costs more, and it adds a model you must look after.

Teaching behaviour and teaching knowledge are different jobs. They need different kinds of data.

Measure every change against real cases with known answers. The next video is Step 1, Instructions.

</Slide>
