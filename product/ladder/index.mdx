<PlainTerms>
When an agent is not good enough there are eight different things you can change, and they are not equally sensible to try. Rewriting the instructions is cheap and often enough. Retraining a model is expensive, slow, and frequently fixes the wrong problem. The first three changes are about the system around the model, and most organisations should live there. The last five change the model itself, and choosing between them is a matter of working out what is actually broken rather than climbing higher. The one thing to remember: teaching a model to behave differently and teaching it about your business are two different jobs needing two different kinds of data, and confusing them is why most model-training projects disappoint.
</PlainTerms>

## Why the order matters

**WHY:** "The model is not good enough" is a conclusion, not a diagnosis. It is usually reached when nobody has measured what is failing. The published evidence runs the other way: prompt and context work keeps yielding gains teams assumed were exhausted, and the expensive interventions often fix something that was never the problem. Climbing early is not only wasted money. It adds weight, lock-in and a maintenance burden to a system whose real fault is still sitting there underneath.

**WHAT:** Treat improvement as two decks. **Deck A changes the system around the model** and is genuinely sequential: exhaust each step before the next is justified. **Deck B changes the model itself** and is not sequential: after the first step you choose by symptom, and several of the options combine rather than replace one another. Everything rests on an <Term k="eval">evaluation</Term> suite you own, because every test below is a measurement.

## The worked example running through these chapters

**In short:** One agent, followed from a first prompt to the top of the ladder, so each step is shown doing real work rather than described in the abstract.

Every chapter applies its step to the same case: a **financial crime investigation agent** at a retail and commercial bank. It assembles the context behind a transaction-monitoring alert, retrieves the customer's history, drafts the narrative, and proposes a disposition. The analyst decides.

The [banking blueprint](/library/blueprints/verticals/banking-and-financial-services) calls this the strongest internal case in the vertical, for three reasons that also make it a good teacher. Alert volume is high, so cost is a real constraint rather than a footnote. The labels are real, because every alert eventually gets a disposition and some become confirmed cases, which means quality can be measured rather than argued about. And the analyst stays the decider, so the agent is assisting rather than acting, which keeps the control question honest.

It also happens to exercise every chapter. The bank has decades of its own written material, which is what makes continued pretraining a genuine option, and its transaction data is a network rather than a document, which is the one situation where the final chapter applies.

## Deck A: change the system

**In short:** Three steps, in order, and where most organisations should stop.

| # | Step | What it changes | Move on when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [Instructions](/ladder/instructions) | The wording of the task | Accuracy climbs for a week of prompt work, then goes flat |
| 2 | [Context](/ladder/context) | What reaches the model: how documents are represented, and what the agent remembers | Your representation beats a <Term k="matched control">matched control</Term>, and what still fails is acting rather than knowing |
| 3 | [Tools and the loop](/ladder/tools-and-the-loop) | The system that runs the model | The loop is instrumented and traced, and the residue is the model not knowing your domain |

Step 3 is the one most often skipped. Teams go from "the context is good and it is still failing" straight to training. The evidence says otherwise: holding task, environment and model constant, harness choices measurably change the decisions an agent makes even where the final success rate looks the same, and in retrieval-heavy work the harness can matter more than the retrieval architecture. Most organisations that believe they need a trained model need better tools and a better loop.

## The distinction that decides Deck B

**In short:** Teaching behaviour and teaching knowledge are different jobs, needing different data, and most disappointment comes from confusing them.

| | Supervised <Term k="fine-tuning">fine-tuning</Term> | Continued pretraining |
|---|---|---|
| Learns from | Input and output pairs | Raw text |
| How much | Thousands of examples | Billions of <Term k="token">tokens</Term> |
| Changes | How the model **behaves** | What the model **knows**, and how it reads your language |
| Wrong tool for | Adding knowledge | Fixing format or tone |

An enterprise with a large proprietary corpus has the raw text and often very few labelled pairs. That makes continued pretraining the natural option for a data-rich organisation, and it is why so many fine-tuning projects disappoint: the wrong instrument was chosen for the complaint.

Facts about particular customers, cases and policies belong in neither column. They stay in retrieval and in the agent's governed memory, where they can be cited, updated and deleted. Weights can do none of those things.

## Deck B: change the model

**In short:** Not a climb. Read the symptom, pick the chapter, and expect to combine more than one.

  title="Deck B branches by symptom"
  caption="Inconsistent behaviour points to adapters. Proven quality with excessive cost points to distillation. Checkable but wrong judgement points to reinforcement. />

| What is failing | Where to go |
|---|---|
| Wrong format, tone, or task behaviour | [Adapters and fine-tuning](/ladder/adapters-and-fine-tuning) |
| Right quality, too slow or too expensive | [Distillation](/ladder/distillation) |
| Right format, wrong judgement, and outcomes can be graded | [Reinforcement fine-tuning](/ladder/reinforcement-fine-tuning) |
| Does not understand your domain's language, documents or notation | [Continued pretraining](/ladder/continued-pretraining) |
| Your data is not text-shaped: graphs, sequences, signals at scale | [Custom pretraining and architecture](/ladder/custom-pretraining) |

These stack. Continued pretraining followed by supervised fine-tuning is a standard pairing rather than an either-or: the first teaches the domain, the second teaches the task. Distillation is not a climb at all, and can follow any of the others.

## The rails: an evaluation suite you own

**In short:** Every test on this page is a measurement, so without an eval suite there is no ladder, only opinion.

<TeachingIllustration
  src="/images/ladder/overview-evaluation-rails-labeled-v2.webp"
  alt="A sealed collection of real cases passes unchanged through repeated evaluation checkpoints; a score beyond the human band triggers an alarm and a return to inspect the grader."
  title="The evaluation suite is the rail beneath every stage"
  caption="The same locked cases and matched controls test each intervention. A gate opens because the evidence justifies a different kind of change, not because a new technique is fashionable. If an automated score escapes beyond careful human performance, inspect the grader before trusting the result."
/>

An organisation that cannot measure its agent will climb on anecdote, and anecdote favours whichever intervention is most fashionable. Build the suite from real failures rather than imagined ones, before the agent rather than after, and have it owned by the domain experts who can say what a correct answer looks like.

If the agent keeps memory, the suite also tests what memory breaks: a fact that changed over time, a question the record cannot answer, a record that was deleted, and a person who may not see it.

One hazard belongs here rather than further up. When a validation score exceeds what a careful person achieves on the same task, the system has learned the rubric rather than the job. Treat that as an alarm, not a result. It is the standard signature of <Term k="reward hacking">reward hacking</Term>, and it matters most on the graded steps in Deck B.

<PrincipleCard eyebrow="The rule" title="Change the system before you change the model" kind="evidence">
Deck A is sequential and cheap, and it is where the evidence says most of the available gain still is. Deck B is expensive, adds a trained artifact you must maintain, and is chosen by symptom rather than by ambition. An organisation that skips the tests usually arrives in Deck B carrying a fault that step 2 or step 3 would have fixed, and now has a model to keep as well.
</PrincipleCard>

<EvidenceBadge status="mixed-evidence" /> Deck A rests partly on our own measurements. Deck B is documented from published work and vendor documentation, with sources and dates on each chapter. The two-deck structure itself is an author position.
