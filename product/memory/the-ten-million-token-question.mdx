An investigation asks whether the same audit issue has appeared across several sites over years. Reading only the latest report misses the pattern. Sending the entire archive into every call creates a different problem: how will the agent locate all the relevant evidence, recognize corrections, and keep the search affordable?

## Size is only one part of difficulty

Ten million tokens is an example scale, not a threshold at which one architecture becomes mandatory. The number of similar records, the need to join events across time, permission boundaries, and the consequences of missing one source can matter as much as total length.

[BEAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27246), first published in 2025 and revised in February 2026, evaluates generated conversations extending to ten million tokens with multiple memory abilities. It is evidence that long histories deserve explicit testing, not proof of performance on a company's operational archive. Reviewed 18 September 2026.

## Scope the search, then challenge the scope

Start with the site, asset class, period, and issue under investigation. Use structured filters and suitable retrieval to assemble candidate evidence. Then test whether those filters excluded related sites, earlier terminology, or an event filed under a different identifier.

For a systemic audit investigation, a match at one site is a lead for further collection. Ask what evidence would support or contradict the proposed pattern. A loop should close a specific gap rather than keep searching without a stopping rule.

## Keep a path from summaries to originals

A site-level summary can help select a year or case to inspect. Preserve links to the underlying records and their versions. A summary of summaries should not become the only surviving account of events.

Use exact queries when an official count or status is requested. Approximate similarity retrieval is not automatically an exhaustive population for a company-wide calculation.

## Updates become part of the workload

New reports arrive, identifiers merge, permissions change, and old sources are corrected. Measure the work of refreshing indexes and derivatives, not just the cost of one query against a frozen archive.

Partitioning by team or site can help scope retrieval, but a cross-site investigation needs an explicit authorized path across those boundaries. Avoid assuming a single global index solves access.

## Grow the test before growing the design

Test increasing corpus sizes while retaining comparable questions and known supporting evidence. Add near-duplicates, older versions, delayed reports, and relevant cases under different wording. Measure important evidence missed as well as irrelevant evidence returned.

Track final decision quality, investigation steps, elapsed time, review burden, and full cost. Keep vendor results separate unless dataset, model, retrieval budget, judge, and scoring rules genuinely match.
