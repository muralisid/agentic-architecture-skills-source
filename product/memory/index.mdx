<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-01">

An AI agent forgets everything when its task ends. Unless something is built to remember for it. That something is called its memory. This section looks at how memories are built today. It looks at how they are scored. And it asks how far the scores are from what an enterprise needs.

</Slide>

</Slide>

## Why memory needs its own section

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-agent">

At the heart of any AI system is a model. It reads text and writes text back. A system that uses a model to do a job is called an agent.

The model keeps nothing between calls. When the task ends, everything it saw is gone. The next task starts from a blank page.

For a chat about the weather, that is fine. For a claims team or a service desk, it is not. They need the agent to remember last week, last year, and the customer's whole history.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-memory">

Memory is what is built around the model to remember for it. It is a store, an index, and a way to bring the right piece back. It is not part of the model.

What the store keeps decides what can ever be recalled. How the index works decides what is found. Those two choices are the subject of this section.

This guide already says what a memory must carry. Its source, its permissions, and a working delete. This section adds how it is built, and whether the build works.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-count">

By September 2026 there are more than thirty named memory systems. Four large cloud providers ship a managed memory service. Six survey papers propose their own taxonomy.

Since 2025 at least ten new benchmarks have appeared. Every system reports a score on one of them. Almost none of the scores can be compared with each other.

The judges differ. The question subsets differ. And the sizes of the tests are far below what an enterprise runs at.

</Slide>

## Two facts and two schools

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-two-facts">

The first fact is what is stored. Original words, a summary, extracted facts, a graph, or the weights. What is stored decides what can ever be found again.

The second fact is when a model runs. At write time, as each document arrives. At read time, as each question comes in. In the background, between tasks. Or during training, so the memory lives in the weights.

Those two facts decide the cost, and they decide what is lost. Page two sorts every named system by them.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-scores">

Vendors report scores in the low nineties on the two best-known benchmarks. Those scores are real, under the judges the vendors chose.

But look at the size of the test. The smaller benchmark has conversations of 16 to 26 thousand tokens. The larger one has about 115 thousand tokens per question.

A modern model reads either of those in one call. So a high score there says little about memory at scale. Page three shows what the benchmarks measure and where scores mislead.

</Slide>

## The size that matters

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-scale">

A token is the unit AI usage is measured in. It is roughly three quarters of a word. Ten million tokens is about seven million words.

That is a little more than one year of one worker's received mail and chat. Before any attachments, documents, tickets or meetings.

A customer's history over a decade is larger. A litigation file is larger. A plant's maintenance record is larger. Ten million is the floor for an enterprise memory, not the ceiling.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-beam">

One public benchmark reaches ten million tokens. It is called BEAM, and it was published at a top conference in 2026. At that size, giving the model everything scores 0.10 on a scale of 0 to 1.

Finding the best pieces first and passing them in scores 0.25.

The benchmark authors built their own memory design. It scores 0.27. That is the best published, independently run result at ten million tokens. Nothing is close to solid.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-vendors">

Three vendors have since published their own ten-million-token numbers. Each used its own judge and its own test harness.

None of them is comparable to the paper's table. None is comparable to the others. None has been reproduced by anyone else.

This section shows them, flagged as vendor reported. It never places them in a ranking.

</Slide>

## How this section handles numbers

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-rules">

This section handles numbers under three rules. First, a number from the party that built the system is flagged as vendor reported. The judges differ, so it is never placed in a ranking with another vendor's number.

Second, a number this guide produced by running the system's own code is marked independently measured. It sits in its own row.

Third, a retrieval score and an answer score are different quantities. One asks whether the right passage was found. The other asks whether the answer came out right. They never share a table. This is the single most useful habit a reader can take from the section.

</Slide>

## The question this section leaves open

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-open">

No independently run, published result shows any memory architecture answering well over ten million tokens. No long-context model does either.

None has earned a recommendation, so this section makes none. It ends on the gap, and leaves the question with you.

Our own research on the question is under way. It will be published here when it is complete, in the same two tables.

</Slide>

## What to remember

<Slide journey="memory/index" id="mo-recap">

Here is what to remember. A memory is decided by what it stores and when a model runs.

Every high score was earned on a test that fits in one call to a model.

Read every number with its unit, its pool and its judge. Keep retrieval scores and answer scores apart.

Ten million tokens is the floor for an enterprise memory. Nothing solid has been shown there, by anyone. The next page sorts the architectures.
