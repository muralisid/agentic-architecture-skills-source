<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-01">

In the spring of 2026 one memory project claimed a perfect score on two of the main memory benchmarks. Perfect scores do not happen on real tests. So rather than argue about it, we ran the project's own code ourselves. On three benchmarks, with no paid model involved. The published numbers came out exactly. Then we looked at what they were numbers of.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-intro">

First, why we chose this system to test. Then what we did, step by step.

Then what the reproduction found, in the two-table form this section uses.

Then the realisation that took longest to see. Last, what it does and does not mean for a reader.

</Slide>

## Why this system

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-claim">

In April 2026 a local-first memory project published 100 percent on the two best-known memory benchmarks. It stores the original text unchanged. It reached 47 thousand GitHub stars within two weeks.

No other system had claimed a perfect score on either benchmark. The strongest vendor claims sat in the low nineties, under judges the vendors chose.

The larger benchmark's own authors had measured a frontier model at 60.6 percent. A perfect score there is either a discovery or a measurement artefact. The difference can be settled by running the code.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-why">

We chose it for three reasons. It made the least realistic claim in the field.

It runs no language model at write time. And none at read time unless an optional reranker is switched on. So every published figure can be reproduced from its own scripts, for no cost.

Its project history records partial corrections on 7 and 14 April 2026. We ran the test on 13 September 2026. The 100 percent rows were still in its published benchmark file that day. So we ran it.

</Slide>

## What we did

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-did">

We ran the project's own evaluation scripts, unmodified, on the two small benchmarks. With no API key.

Then we reimplemented its code path so each component could be switched on and off. We validated the reimplementation against the published figures to the decimal.

Then we took the same code path and weights to the ten-million-token benchmark. The project had not run it.

Every number was recomputed independently from the per-question records. The whole run reproduces from a clean clone by one command. The full piece, with code and outputs, sits in the research library.

</Slide>

## What we found

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-lme">

On the larger benchmark, their script produced their headline 96.6 exactly. That is any-hit recall at five: was one labelled session in the top five? The unit is a session, and the pool is 48 sessions per question.

On the same ranking, the standard TREC fraction is 91.7.

Their keyword-boosted mode, with no rerank, reaches 98.2 any-hit and 94.3 as the fraction.

The published 100 rests on a paid rerank plus three fixes written for three named failing questions. We did not run it; it needs a key. The project's own held-out figure for that mode is 98.4.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-locomo">

On the smaller benchmark, raw mode scores 60.3 at k of ten. The unit is a session and the pool is 28 per question.

The keyword-boosted mode scores 88.9.

And at k of 50, every configuration scores 100. On every one of the 1,986 questions, with no reranker and no model. The largest conversation has 32 sessions. The published setting asked for the top 50. The published claim credited a paid reranker; the reranker had nothing left to do.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-beam">

Then we took the same code to the ten-million-token benchmark. The unit is a message, and the pool is about 21 thousand messages per question. In the top ten it finds 24.5 percent of the evidence. Under the project's own any-hit form, 41.5.

In the top hundred, 42.3 percent as the fraction, and 65.9 as any-hit.

The ceiling given the number of gold messages per question is 91.6 at k of ten. So the shortfall is headroom, not arithmetic.

</Slide>

## Three mechanisms, all measured

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-truncation">

Three mechanisms, all measured, explain the numbers. The first is truncation. The default encoder reads the first 256 word pieces of a session, about 190 words.

On the larger benchmark, 88 percent of the evidence sits inside that window. The benchmark places the answer early in a constructed session.

On the smaller benchmark, 54 percent sits outside it. For those questions the encoder scores 39.7 against a chance baseline of 37.1. The keyword scorer reads the full text. That is what carries the smaller benchmark from 60.3 to 88.9.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-metric">

The second mechanism is the metric. The 96.6 is any-hit. A full hit if one gold session is in the top five, however many there were.

As the standard fraction on the same ranking, it is 91.7.

The 100 on the smaller benchmark is a k of 50 over pools of at most 32. It measures nothing about ranking.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-density">

The third mechanism is density. At ten million tokens the pool is 21 thousand messages.

Word overlap between question and evidence predicts recall at a correlation of about 0.5. Message length does not.

A larger encoder moves the result by about a point. Splitting messages into smaller pieces lowers it. Keyword search alone, with no encoder, scores within four points of the full pipeline.

</Slide>

## The realisation

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-realisation">

This is what took longest to see. The project's page presents its results the way the answer-scoring vendors present theirs. But its scripts embed the question, rank the sessions, and check whether a labelled session is in the top k.

No answer is generated. So no answer is judged. The optional model step is a selector. It is asked which passage most directly answers the question, from a 300-character prefix of each. It is not a reader.

The project's own benchmark file says so, below its tables. The scores do not measure whether a model can answer the question. Read as retrieval, the work is sound. Read as accuracy, the 100 percent is not valid. A memory system that produces no answer cannot claim accuracy at all.

</Slide>

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-sound">

Read as what it is, the work is sound. A verbatim store with a keyword channel is a real and useful baseline. Free to index, and honest about what it keeps. On the small benchmarks it is hard to beat by anything that transforms the text.

Read as what a reader assumes, an accuracy of 100 percent, it is not valid. Retrieval recall is always higher than the end-to-end score.

One of the two 100 figures returns the entire candidate pool. The other rests on a paid reranker and three fixes for three named questions. The project itself calls those fixes teaching to the test. That is the finding this guide takes forward. It is why the benchmarks page keeps the two schools in separate tables.

</Slide>

## What it does and does not mean

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-means">

It does not mean the project misled anyone deliberately. Its file says what its metric is, and it retracted several claims in April 2026. It does not mean the system is recommended, or that any other is. The standing rule holds: run your own acceptance suite on your own corpus, and score the answers.

It does mean three things a reader can use. A perfect score on a memory benchmark is a signal. Look at the metric, the k and the pool first.

A system's own no-model retrieval score, honestly labelled, is the right baseline to hold every extracted or graph architecture against.

And at ten million tokens the same code finds a quarter of the evidence. That is where the open question starts. Our own architecture and its results on these three benchmarks are not published here yet. When the study is complete, they will appear on this page in the same two tables.

</Slide>

## What to remember

<Slide journey="memory/what-we-have-measured" id="mm-recap">

Here is what to remember. We reproduced a 100 percent claim exactly, from the project's own code.

It was a retrieval score with no answer behind it, at a k that returned the whole pool.

Three measured mechanisms explain the rest: truncation, the metric, and density.

At ten million tokens the same code finds a quarter of the evidence. The sources page carries every reference for this section, with its date and its vendor flag.

</Slide>

## The numbers behind the slides

### What we found
**In short:** Every published retrieval figure reproduced exactly. The 100 percent figures reproduced too, and turned out to mean something other than what a reader would assume.

Retrieval-only school. The unit is a session on the two small benchmarks and a message on BEAM; the candidate pool per question is on every row.

| Benchmark | Unit, pool | Configuration | Metric | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LongMemEval_S | session, 48 | raw, no model | recall at 5, any-hit, the project's headline form | 96.6 |
| LongMemEval_S | session, 48 | same ranking | recall at 5, TREC fraction | 91.7 |
| LongMemEval_S | session, 48 | keyword-boosted, no rerank | recall at 5, any-hit and TREC | 98.2 and 94.3 |
| LongMemEval_S | session, 48 | keyword-boosted plus a paid rerank and three fixes for three named questions | recall at 5 | the published 100; not run, needs a key; the project's own held-out figure is 98.4 |
| LoCoMo | session, 28 | raw, no model | recall at 10, fraction | 60.3 |
| LoCoMo | session, 28 | keyword-boosted, no model | recall at 10, fraction | 88.9 |
| LoCoMo | session, 28 | any configuration, no model, no rerank | recall at 50 | 100.0 |
| BEAM 10M | message, about 21,000 | keyword-boosted, no model | recall at 10, fraction and any-hit | 24.5 and 41.5 |
| BEAM 10M | message, about 21,000 | same | recall at 100, fraction and any-hit | 42.3 and 65.9 | The LoCoMo 100 percent reproduces on every one of the 1,986 questions with no reranker and no model, because the largest conversation has 32 sessions and the published setting asked for the top 50.
