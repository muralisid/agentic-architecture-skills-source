<PlainTerms>
A memory benchmark gives a system a long conversation and then asks questions about it. There are two ways to score the result. One checks whether the right passage was found. The other checks whether the final answer was right. They are different questions with different numbers, and most published claims quote one while the reader assumes the other. This page sets out the benchmarks, who scores above 90 on which, and how a score can be true and still mislead. The one thing to remember: a memory score without its unit, its candidate pool and its judge is not a number, it is a headline.
</PlainTerms>

## Two schools of scoring

**In short:** Retrieval-only scoring asks whether the gold evidence reached the top k. Full question-answering scoring asks whether the answer came out right. Neither is the lesser; they are never merged.

The retrieval school measures recall at k: of the units marked as evidence for a question, what fraction sits in the top k the system returns. The standard instrument is the TREC definition, computed by tools such as pytrec_eval. A looser variant, any-hit, scores a full hit when at least one gold unit is in the top k; it is always higher on questions with more than one gold unit. The <Term k="retrieval">retrieval</Term> school is honest about what it leaves out: no answer is generated, so nothing is known about whether a reader could use what was found.

The answer school runs a reader over what was retrieved and scores the answer, by token overlap against a reference or by a model acting as judge. This is what the LoCoMo and LongMemEval papers score. It depends on the reader and on the judge, and there is no standard judge prompt, so two systems scored by two vendors are not on the same scale even when the benchmark is the same.

Both schools are legitimate. The only error is to place a retrieval-only number beside an answer number and read them as one thing. Every table below says which school it belongs to.

Each reports the same two things in its own words: plain retrieval or plain long context remains competitive with dedicated memory systems, and strong scores on the factual benchmarks do not carry over to using the memory well. ## Why the two small benchmarks are saturated

**In short:** A LoCoMo conversation fits inside a modern context window several times over, and both benchmarks are measured under judges the vendors choose.

LoCoMo's ten released conversations run 16 to 26 thousand tokens each. Every frontier model reads that in one call, and the largest vendor's own paper reports a full-context baseline of 72.90 against its best memory variant at 68.44. On top of that, the released data has known defects. The scorer's code treats category 1 as multi-hop while the paper lists single-hop first, so vendor per-category tables disagree with each other on which category is which. The 446 adversarial questions have no gold answer, so vendors drop them and score 1,540. An audit in April 2026 found 99 score-corrupting labels among those 1,540, a theoretical ceiling near 93 under the released answers, and 45 of 47 commonsense questions in one conversation with the answer field set to undefined. The vendors reporting 92 to 95 are therefore at or above the ceiling the data allows, each under its own judge prompt. <EvidenceBadge status="mixed-evidence" />

LongMemEval is the better-designed benchmark: a fixed judge model with over 97 percent agreement with human graders, seven question types including knowledge updates and abstention, and separate columns for retrieval recall and answer accuracy. Its authors measured a frontier model falling from 0.870 with only the evidence sessions to 0.606 with the full 115 thousand token history. Vendors have since reported 90 to 94 with their own readers and judges, and retrieval-only scores of 96.6 to 100 with no model at all. The benchmark's mean candidate pool is 48 sessions, so a retrieval score at k of 50 covers the whole pool for most questions and measures nothing. <EvidenceBadge status="primary-source" />

## The dispute of 2025, as documented

The two largest memory vendors published each other's scores. In April 2025 one reported the other at 65.99 on LoCoMo. In May 2025 the other replied that it had erred in its own earlier claim of 84 and corrected itself to 75.14, while criticising the first for misconfiguration. The first vendor's chief technology officer then filed a public issue recomputing the second at 58.44, on the grounds that adversarial answers had been counted in the numerator of a score whose denominator excluded them, that prompts had been changed, and that one run had been compared against ten. The issue was closed without a reply on the thread. Both vendors now publish scores above 90 on the same benchmark under newer judges. None of these numbers is wrong on its own terms. Together they show that the benchmark cannot separate the two systems. <EvidenceBadge status="vendor-reported" />

## Ten ways a published score misleads

Each item below has a primary source in the [sources](/memory/sources) page. None requires bad faith; most are choices a benchmark leaves open.

1. **Any-hit reported as recall.** A full hit if one gold unit is in the top k. On the same ranking, one system's 96.6 any-hit at k of 5 is 91.7 as the TREC fraction.
2. **k larger than the candidate pool.** At k of 50 over conversations of 19 to 32 sessions, every session is returned and recall is 1.0 by construction. A 100 percent claim was published on exactly this setting.
3. **Retrieval-only beside judged answers.** A retrieval score is always higher than the end-to-end score on the same system. Placing a 96 from one school beside a 72 from the other reads as a 24-point lead that does not exist.
4. **The credited target.** A May 2026 study showed that moving credit between the raw turn, the source session and a canonical form changes nDCG on 83 to 94 percent of queries and flips the order of systems.
5. **Category relabelling and denominators.** LoCoMo's paper order and code order differ; three vendors map the categories three ways; one dispute turned on adversarial answers in a numerator whose denominator excluded them.
6. **Fixes tuned to named questions.** One vendor's own file records three fixes written for three failing questions and labels them "teaching to the test"; held-out, the 100 became 98.4.
7. **The vendor as reader and judge.** One vendor scores with the same frontier model as reader and as judge; another scores its managed platform with proprietary components. There is no standard judge prompt.
8. **Corrupted or missing gold.** 446 LoCoMo questions have no answer; 99 of the remaining 1,540 have labels that penalise correct answers; token F1 scores "7 May 2023" against "May 7th, 2023" as different.
9. **Single runs.** One vendor published one run against a competitor's ten with standard deviations.
10. **Synthetic recall as a proxy for reading.** Needle-in-haystack tests do not predict downstream performance; on the hardest lexical-mismatch test, eleven of thirteen models lose more than half their accuracy by 32 thousand tokens.

## How to read a memory claim

**In short:** Ask five questions before believing a number, and the fifth is the one vendors rarely answer.

1. Which school: was an answer generated and judged, or was a passage found?
2. What unit was retrieved, a session, a turn or a message, and how many candidates were in the pool?
3. What k, and is k smaller than the pool?
4. Who was the reader and who was the judge, and was either the vendor's choice?
5. How many tokens was the corpus, and is that within a single context window of the reader?

A claim that answers all five and still stands is worth a reproduction. A claim that answers fewer than three is a headline.
