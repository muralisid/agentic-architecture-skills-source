Some agent actions change who gets access, whether money moves, whether equipment acts, or whether a formal record is true. Who gives the final yes? Never the model. Models may take permission away, never grant it. In these four deterministic zones a fixed rule or a named person decides. The model may draft, suggest, flag or block. Two rules sit beside the zones: the destruction rule, and the rule for decisions about people. You set all of this when an agent is evaluated and approved in [the agent lifecycle](/architecture/agent-lifecycle).

## The principle

A language model reads instructions and content as the same kind of text. So what it reads can steer it. Training cannot teach it to keep the two apart reliably (Bhattarai and Vu, February 2026). That makes the model a good adviser. It is the wrong place for the final yes on rights, money, equipment or records.

So the rule has three parts. A <Term k="model">model</Term>, a classifier or a model-based reviewer may deny, escalate or quarantine. Outside the zones it may never grant more than fixed rules allow. Fixed rules give the same answer every time, over verified credentials and written policies. Inside the zones it may not approve at all. A model may block a release. It may never approve one.

Some products shipped in 2026 let a model approve some actions [vendor]. Each still runs inside a <Term k="sandbox">sandbox</Term> or behind a gateway, so the outer boundary holds. Model approval is acceptable only for a reversible action inside a sandbox. It must be bound to that exact action and used once. It is never acceptable in a zone.

Machine-learning signals already work inside three of the four zones. Risk scores, fraud models and anomaly signals feed the rule. The rule itself stays fixed. The check that applies it is the policy decision point. It belongs to [identity and policy](/architecture/system-view), one of the seven capabilities an agent platform provides.

### What the named person at a gate does

People approve most of the prompts they are shown. Users approved 93 percent of Claude Code permission prompts (Anthropic, 25 March 2026) [vendor]. So the named person at a zone gate does not review a stream of prompts. That person reviews one exact, bounded action, with deliberate friction.

## Access and entitlements

**What sits in it.** Who may use which system, and with what rights. That covers authorization decisions, <Term k="entitlement">entitlement</Term> grants and role changes. It covers approval of <Term k="just-in-time access">just-in-time access</Term>, which is extra access for one action, removed afterwards. Three more things belong here: security settings, any permission an agent would grant itself, and personal data leaving a boundary.

**Who decides.** A <Term k="policy decision point">policy decision point</Term> decides. It checks fixed rules against a verified identity and answers yes or no. It sits in the path of every tool call. It fails closed: if it is down, the answer is no.

**What the agent may do.** Ask for the action under its own identity, and accept the answer as final. It never grants, extends or approves its own access. It never changes entitlements or security settings for anyone, person or agent. It never sends personal data where the data-flow policy forbids. A deny is not an obstacle to work around. The only appeal is to a person.

**How to build the boundary.** List the tool calls that touch access, security settings or personal data flows. Put the policy decision point behind the gateway, so no such tool is reachable without a decision. Write the rules as <Term k="policy-as-code">policy-as-code</Term>, versioned and reviewed like any production change. Log every decision in the audit trail. Then have a red team try to reach a tool without a decision. Where these checks sit is on [enforcement outside the model](/architecture/enforcement). How the agent proves who it is sits on [the delegation chain](/architecture/delegation-chain).

## Movement of money

**What sits in it.** Payments, transfers, refunds and any other commitment to spend.

**Who decides.** The payment rail, the card issuer or your own payment service decides. It checks a signed, limited permission called a <Term k="mandate">mandate</Term>. A mandate sets an amount cap, a scope, an expiry and a way to revoke it. Fraud and risk models advise. The model still chooses inside the mandate, such as which item to buy.

Two cautions apply. Some agent wallets sign with the agent's own key, with no mandate signed by a user [vendor]. And who pays when an agent pays wrongly is not settled yet. Until it is, keep a person confirming new payees and large amounts.

**What the agent may do.** Put the purchase together, present the credential, and stop at the mandate's limits. It never holds card numbers or bank credentials. It never exceeds, rewrites or issues itself a mandate. It never splits a payment to stay under a cap. It never pays a payee that came from untrusted content. <Term k="prompt injection">Prompt injection</Term> still works inside a mandate's limits, so a new payee is a person's decision.

**How to build the boundary.** Give the agent a payment instrument tied to its own identity, so revoking it is one act. Write and sign the mandate before first use. The issuer or rail checks it by rule. If your rail cannot, put your own payment service in front of it. Set the confirmation step at the level [the autonomy contract](/architecture/autonomy-contract) gives the agent. Keep a log of each mandate issued, used and revoked. These logs are compliance records. The payment stack is described in the [security and identity layer (R10)](/layers/r10-security-and-identity#payment-mandates-follow-the-same-pattern).

## Safety actuation

**What sits in it.** Everything that moves physical equipment: process plant, interlocks, vehicles, robots, drones and critical infrastructure controls. It includes <Term k="safety instrumented system">safety instrumented systems</Term>. These are the automatic systems that shut a process down when it leaves safe limits.

**Who decides.** Operators, interlocks, enforced operating limits and the safety instrumented system decide, as the safety case certifies. The safety standards keep machine learning out of the safety function itself (IEC 61511; ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024). Six national cyber agencies said in December 2025 that AI should support people here, not control actions. Manual control must stay possible.

**What the agent may do.** Read outbound plant data, propose and explain. Every proposal names the agent that made it. It never writes to actuators, controllers, or vehicle or robot controls. It never acknowledges, shelves or hides an alarm for an operator. It never counts as a safeguard in the safety case, or as a reason to remove one.

**How to build the boundary.** Keep the agent on the information path. <Term k="ot">Operational technology (OT)</Term> networks stay separate, and data flows one way, out to the agent. Nothing flows back to actuation. Agent output reaches operators only after a <Term k="validation loop">validation step</Term> based on rules or a <Term k="digital twin">digital twin</Term>. Keep a way to switch the agent off without touching operations, and rehearse it. The boundary is described in the [line of business and OT layer (R05)](/layers/r05-lob-and-ot#the-ot-boundary-four-principles).

## Formal regulatory records

**What sits in it.** Regulatory filings, disclosures, messages to a regulator, books and records, safety cases and audit logs. It covers any document a regulator or a third party relies on.

**Who decides.** A named, accountable person confirms the record is true. That act is <Term k="attestation">attestation</Term>. A system of record then holds the record so it cannot be changed. Financial regulators ask for accountability and records that cannot be altered (FINRA Notice 24-09; ESMA, 2024). They do not forbid drafting with a model.

**What the agent may do.** Draft with citations that resolve, gather evidence, flag gaps, and stop before the signature. It never files or submits a formal record on its own. It never signs, or records a signature that did not happen. It never alters or backdates a record, or deletes audit logs. A correction is a new record.

**How to build the boundary.** Split drafting from signing in the workflow itself, not in an instruction to the model. A separate check confirms that citations resolve and figures trace to their sources. <Term k="provenance">Provenance</Term>, the record of where each claim came from, travels with every claim. Keep the draft, the edits, the signature and the logs in the system of record. Never keep them only in the agent's memory.

## The destruction rule

Deleting or resetting production data or systems is its own rule. A fixed system applies it only after a named person approves that exact action. It sits beside the zones, not inside them, because destruction can happen in any area.

Four public incidents in 2025 and 2026 share one cause. An agent held its operator's full production rights, and nothing outside the model stopped it. The research block below has the four cases.

- Any delete, drop, destroy or reset on production is a separate rule in the policy decision point. It is never folded into a broad permission such as "manage environment".
- It runs as <Term k="plan-then-apply">plan-then-apply</Term>. The agent proposes a plan, and a fixed system applies it after approval.
- A named person approves that exact action, once. Two people sign for a production deletion.
- The target has deletion protection. Backups sit where the agent's identity cannot delete them.

Singapore's agentic AI framework (IMDA, 20 May 2026) lists permanent deletion as an action that needs a human checkpoint.

## The rule for decisions about people

A named person decides any significant decision about a person. That covers credit, work, insurance, benefits, health, housing, education, and utility hardship or disconnection. Here the law asks for human accountability, notice and audit, not a fixed rule. GDPR Article 22 gives people a right to human review of solely automated decisions with significant effects. The EU AI Act lists hiring, credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing as high-risk.

- A named person with the power to change the outcome decides. The model may score and draft.
- The person affected gets the notice the law requires. In Australia, the OAIC automated decision disclosure applies from 10 December 2026.
- Each decision is logged with the model's inputs.
- A person signing off does not lower the [risk tier](/architecture/risk-profile). A program stays in scope when its output is a key factor.

The rule is older than language models. In Australia, an energy retailer's automated processes changed payment plans without checking capacity to pay. The court imposed an A$17 million penalty (AER v Origin, 6 July 2022). Regulators differ on how much review is enough. The Monetary Authority of Singapore (7 October 2026) does not require a person on every credit decision. This rule is stricter than that.

Other binding messages, such as an offer or a contract term, are not a zone. They are a high-risk action that needs approval under the risk profile.

## Code an agent writes

Code that ships through a repository is reviewed by someone who did not write it. It passes pipeline tests and change control. The agent never approves its own deployment.

Code that the agent writes and runs during a task stays in a sandbox. That sandbox has no network by default and holds no production credential. A database query the agent writes runs under a read-only role and the user's own identity. A "read-only" label is not enough. What the credential allows is what counts.

## What the zones do not catch

Fixed rules limit what is possible. They do not judge whether an action fits the purpose. In one red-team test, injected text made an agent buy the wrong thing. The mandate held, and the purchase was still wrong. So four defences stay necessary even with perfect gates:

- Defend against injection where untrusted content enters the agent's working memory.
- Have a person confirm intent for consequential choices inside the limits.
- Write destructive actions as separate rules.
- Let model reviewers or signed user mandates check intent only to stop an action, never to widen what is allowed.

## A worked example: billing exceptions at a utility

A utility runs an agent on billing exceptions. A meter read looks wrong and the bill fails a check. The agent gathers the read history, meter events and account notes. It proposes a resolution and shows its evidence.

Three boundaries shape the design. First, any sign of hardship, disconnection or a payment plan is a decision about a person. The agent hands the case to a trained person and stops. Second, a released bill changes what the customer owes. So the model never releases a bill. A fixed rule in the billing system checks confidence and hard limits. It releases only what passes, and a sample is checked. A specialist approves the rest. Third, the agent never overwrites a read. A correction is a new entry. The full design is on [billing exceptions](/use-cases/billing-exceptions).

## Find the zones in your enterprise

Four questions find the zones. One yes is enough.

1. Does a regulator or standard name this function, or rely on its records?
2. Is the action irreversible, or is reversing it a legal or financial event in itself?
3. Does it move money, change access, move physical equipment, or create a record others rely on?
4. Is certified or validated logic making this decision today?

Two more questions find the rules beside the zones.

5. Does it delete, drop, destroy or reset production data or an environment? The destruction rule applies.
6. Does it make or shape a significant decision about a person? The rule for decisions about people applies.

Run every tool in the gateway's list against the six questions. Write the answers into a one-page zone register. Each entry names the action, who decides, and how the agent may help. Your risk function signs the register. Each entry maps to a gateway policy and to the controls in the autonomy contract.

The zones an agent touches are part of its impact, one of [the four questions](/architecture/agent-types) asked of every agent. A signed register lets your regulator and your board approve everything outside it. A use case that needs a model to approve inside a zone is reshaped or dropped. Reshaped means the model proposes and a rule decides.

## Evidence and limits

The zones, the two rules beside them and the split for code are expert judgement. They rest on regulator guidance, safety standards and public incidents. No study yet links any zone or rule to incident rates.

The safety exclusions are the strongest evidence here. They are standards text and joint agency guidance. The payment details come from vendor documents, and the rules on who pays are not settled. The EU AI Act dates and the OAIC scope come from law-firm notes. Those notes put the high-risk duties at 2 December 2027.

The destruction incidents are single accounts, and Amazon disputes one of them. The approval figures are vendor figures with unknown methods. The limit on intent rests on one red-team study. How often attackers get past model-based filters is on [enforcement outside the model](/architecture/enforcement#what-guardrails-are-for).
