## Two axes, applied per workload

**Autonomy** (who executes, what the human holds): A0 manual, A1 assisted, A2 delegated tasks, A3 supervised autonomy, A4 managed autonomy, A5 governed lights-out. **Learning** (whether and under what control the agent improves): L0 fixed policy, L1 curated learning, L2 governed learning (the flywheel), L3 continuous learning inside guardrails. They move independently; every workload sits somewhere on the grid deliberately, and movement is earned and reversible.

## The controls column is the contract

| Level | Human role | Required controls |
|---|---|---|
| A2 | Review every output | Task scoping, output review, audit |
| A3 | Approve irreversible actions, handle exceptions | Approval gates, evals, budgets, kill switch |
| A4 | Manage exception queues, supervise | Eval-gated change, concern register enforced, supervisor dashboards, designed oversight capacity |
| A5 | Set policy, own accountability, audit outcomes | Proven eval maturity, continuous assurance, regulator-ready evidence, oversight capacity measured in production |

Three rules connect the axes. **A4 and above require L2 or better**: at that level reliability must be improvable, not merely observable. **A4 and above require proven oversight capacity**: a level you cannot supervise is a level you have not reached. And **without a learning loop you have a fixed-policy agent, not a teammate**; fixed policy is the right choice for many workloads, named honestly.

## Readiness gates autonomy

Six dimensions, each scored 0 to 3 (absent, ad hoc, managed, operated): data readiness (including ACL-aware retrievability), integration readiness (governed API coverage, tool exposure, event availability), identity readiness (first-class principals, on-behalf-of flows, secrets never held by agents), operational discipline (observability, offline and online evals, incident practice), governance and value discipline (intake with kill criteria, per-run cost visibility, the two-estate view), and operating capacity. The profile governs, never the total:

<GuideFigure id="readiness-six-dimension-profile" fullWidth />

| Profile | Safe ceiling |
|---|---|
| Any dimension at 0 for the target workload | A1 only |
| Data and integration at 2+, others at 1+ | A2 |
| Data, integration, identity, and operations at 2+ | A3 |
| All six at 2+, operations and governance at 3 | A4, and L2 governed learning is required |
| All six at 3, plus regulator-ready evidence | A5 candidacy, per domain only |

Identity gaps cap autonomy without blocking starts; a workload can begin at A1 while the chain is built.

## The oversight gate, as a burst rate

No credible published human-to-agent supervision ratio exists from any source; the vendor that coined the metric has published no number in two annual editions. What transfers instead is queueing arithmetic and alarm-management standards:

<GuideFigure id="oversight-burst-capacity" fullWidth />

- Capacity follows the fan-out relation: neglect time over interaction time **plus wait time**. Ignoring wait time overstates capacity by up to 67%, and by 36% even in exception-based designs.
- Budgets are set as burst rates per ten minutes, never daily averages; the alarm-management standards hold that a per-ten-minute rate cannot validly be converted to a longer window, and removed their per-day metric because averaging destroys its meaning. A daily-average target will be met on the dashboard and violated in every burst.
- Expect calibrated oversight, not a ratchet: with experience, standing permission broadens **and** intervention rates rise. In measured deployments, auto-approval roughly doubled while interrupt rates nearly doubled alongside it. Rising interventions with expanding autonomy is the system working; falling interventions with expanding autonomy is the thing to investigate.

Instrument per [R13](/layers/r13-operating-model): intervention rate and trend, escalation mix by trigger, wait time per item, verification cost per review, periodic blind checks against self-report.
