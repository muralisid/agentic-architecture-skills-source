**No credible published number exists.** The vendor that coined "human-agent ratio" as a management metric has published no figure in two annual editions. Anyone quoting one is extrapolating.

<GuideFigure id="oversight-burst-capacity" fullWidth />

What the adjacent evidence does say:

- Capacity follows a fan-out relation: how long an agent runs unattended, divided by the attention each check costs **plus the waiting**. Wait time is the term everyone forgets and the one that usually binds.
- The only directly measured supervisory capacity in a comparable task was four to six units, in a low-stakes simulation. A floor for intuition, not a target.
- Consequence-heavy domains regulate below one-to-one: nuclear control rooms require several licensed operators per unit.
- The process industries supply the only standards-grade workload figure: more than one alarm per operator per minute is treated as very likely unacceptable.

**Use the burst rule.** Set supervision capacity as a burst rate over ten minutes, never a daily average. The alarm-management standards dropped their per-day metric because averaging destroys the meaning: any "N agents per reviewer" target set as a daily average will be met on the dashboard and violated in every burst.

And expect the counterintuitive shape: as your people gain trust in an agent, they grant it broader standing permission **and intervene more often**, not less. Rising intervention alongside expanding autonomy is the system working. Falling intervention with expanding autonomy is the thing to investigate.
