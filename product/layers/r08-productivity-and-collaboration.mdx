The productivity and collaboration layer (R08) is the office suite: email, calendars, documents, chat and meetings. It is one of the [fourteen layers](/layers) you own. Today it holds Microsoft 365 or similar, spreadsheets that track exceptions, and the first assistant pilots. It is where most staff meet an agent first, and where the most company data passes through one. For agents, this layer must provide four things. Every agent gets its own <Term k="agent identity">agent identity</Term> with a named sponsor. A mailbox or meeting seat needs a separate <Term k="agent user account">agent user account</Term>, granted rarely. Shared files and chats are permissioned before an assistant can search them. Staff use of personal AI accounts is governed through identity, not by blocking. Rollout then follows the task, because the measured gains are in work people do alone.

## What agents add

<GuideFigure id="layer-08-hero" fullWidth />

### Every agent gets its own identity

In Microsoft's design, an agent identity is a service principal in the enterprise directory. A service principal is the directory's account type for software, not people. It carries a sponsor field: the person or group accountable for the agent. The sponsor is also the contact if a security incident happens. From July 2026, every new agent must have an agent identity, with no opt-out [vendor]. Creating agent identities uses a permission that is granted automatically and cannot be revoked. It is capped at 250 agent identities per blueprint, the template an agent is built from [vendor]. Agent identities are deleted when the agent is deleted, so orphan accounts do not pile up. An agent cannot exist without an identity, so the inventory is complete by design. On the [delegation chain](/architecture/delegation-chain), this is the agent's own identity (ID2).

### A mailbox or meeting seat is a separate grant

An agent user account is a different object. It is an optional user account tied one to one to the agent. It exists only where the agent needs to act as a user. It brings a mailbox, a calendar, a licence, a place in the HR system or a meeting-roster seat. Creating one needs a permission that an administrator grants and can revoke [vendor]. No user account is created automatically. The agent's own identity plus an agent user account is ID3 on the delegation chain.

An agent can be named without a user account. Its display name shows in Teams, Outlook and admin screens. It can be @mentioned in Teams and Slack without one. A user account also does not buy accountability, because the sponsor field already carries it. Nor does it change how people work. One study compared an always-present agent with one called on demand (AIES 2026, 157 people). Trust did not shift much, and accountability stayed with the human expert. So grant an agent user account per need, rarely, with a recorded reason.

### Shared content is permissioned first

An assistant can surface any file its user can open. Field evaluations record assistants surfacing files users should never have seen. That is oversharing from the [data platform layer (R02)](/layers/r02-data-platform), showing up here first. So fix permissions on files, sites and chats before deployment, not after. Take a water utility's billing exceptions as an example. The trackers often live in Excel, email and Teams. An agent that reads them needs its own identity and the same access rules as a person.

### Personal AI accounts are governed through identity

Blocking moves usage rather than stopping it. About 90 percent of organisations block at least one generative AI app [vendor]. Yet 47 percent of users reach AI tools through personal, unmanaged accounts [vendor]. An average organisation records 223 incidents a month of sensitive data sent to AI apps [vendor]. Connector permissions appear as API permissions that conditional access can target. Conditional access is the directory's set of rules on who may reach what, and from where. So the policy engine that governs staff sign-ins also governs what an agent can reach. Make the approved path easier than the personal account.

### Roll out by task

<TeachingIllustration src="/images/layers/r08-task-team-labeled-v1.webp" title="Deploy assistants by task, not by licence count" alt="Two panels. Measured gains so far come from work people do alone, such as email. Meetings and shared documents change only when the team agrees new ways of working. So route solitary, single-author work to the assistant by default. Route shared work there only with a decision to change the team's norms. Drafting and summarising gain time. Data analysis and scheduling lost time in one government evaluation. Measure each task's outcome and keep what improves it.

### Meetings need consent

Notetaking is the most common use of agents in collaboration tools. Capture consent and disclose recording by default. Keep meeting records with their consent records. Align retention with recording law in each place. Two US class actions over meeting recording are active, one filed in July 2026.

### The assistant's mode is a setting

An assistant can rewrite people's ideas, or draw them out with questions. Rewriting improved quality but cut the variety of ideas and people's sense of ownership. Asking questions improved quality and kept both (486 participants, checked again with 640). So set the mode per surface and record it as configuration that can be reviewed. Use questions wherever a variety of contributions matters.

### Review is shallow, so controls must not rely on it

In five-hour coding tasks, 94 percent of developers missed deliberate sabotage inserted by an agent. When a safety monitor flagged it, 56 percent still accepted the malicious code. So assume review effort is low when sizing controls. Judge oversight on how many errors people catch, not on their confidence or speed. Treat any control that raises confidence without raising accuracy as a step backwards.

## What must be true before an agent touches it

Before an agent reads a mailbox, a file or a meeting, these must hold.

- **It has its own identity and a sponsor.** The agent's own identity (ID2) is the minimum. No agent uses a colleague's account.
- **Its connectors are scoped.** Conditional access targets its connector permissions.
- **Any user account has a recorded reason.** An administrator grants it per need and can revoke it.
- **Content permissions are fixed first.** Files, sites and chats are permissioned before the assistant can search them.
- **Meeting consent is captured.** Recording is disclosed, and retention follows recording law in each place.
- **Its output is labelled.** No agent presents as a colleague without saying it is an agent.
- **It is on the lifecycle.** It has a registry record, a sponsor and a periodic review, as the [agent lifecycle](/architecture/agent-lifecycle) sets out. The [governance, risk and sovereignty layer (R11)](/layers/r11-governance-risk-sovereignty) keeps the registry.
- **A new grant is scored again.** A new tool grant changes its permissions, so its [risk profile](/architecture/risk-profile) is scored again.

The enforcement point is the directory and its conditional-access rules, not a proxy blocklist. The [security and identity layer (R10)](/layers/r10-security-and-identity) runs that policy engine. Policy sits outside the model, as [enforcement outside the model](/architecture/enforcement) describes.

## Choices and their verdicts

Each choice is the common choice, tested against the simplest credible alternative. The full records are in the [decision catalog](/decisions).

- **[One assistant or many specialised agents](/decisions#cd-23) (CD-23):** the wrong question. Ask whether the work is solitary or shared, and whether the team will change how it works.
- **[One agent or many](/decisions#cd-21) (CD-21):** a separate design choice. Several agents pay off only for wide, independent work.
- **[Agents as workforce or as tools](/decisions#cd-22) (CD-22):** keep the accountability and drop the personnel metaphor. The sponsor sits on the identity, and agents need no employee records.

## Concerns this layer must honour

The ten cross-cutting concerns are set out in the [concern matrix](/architecture/concern-matrix). This layer carries a specific duty for seven of them.

- **Identity and access:** agent identity is mandatory and automatic. User accounts are granted per need, with a reason.
- **Observability:** actions per active user per day, outcomes by task type, and signs of personal-account use.
- **Traceability and audit:** meeting records are kept with their consent records.
- **Grounding:** permissions on files, sites and chats are fixed before deployment.
- **Impersonation:** agent output is labelled in shared spaces.
- **Privacy:** notetaker consent, limits on monitoring workers, and leakage through personal accounts.
- **Cost:** usage is measured against licence spend, and tasks that lose time are stopped.

## Evidence and limits

The pay-off evidence is consistent: the more rigorous the method, the smaller the effect. The largest field experiment covered 66 firms and 7,137 workers for six months. Users spent two fewer hours a week on email. No other change in the amount or mix of work was detected. Meetings and document counts showed no measurable effect.

UK government evaluations show the same pattern. Self-reported savings came to 26 minutes a day. A comparison study at the Department for Work and Pensions found 19, and a randomised one at HMRC about 12. Danish data showed no effect on earnings or hours. A Department for Business and Trade evaluation observed tasks with six people per group. Summarising got much faster, while Excel analysis got slower and less accurate.

Reviewers miss most deliberate errors, but the sabotage study is a preprint. A pooled analysis of 106 studies found people and AI together did worse than the better of the two. The losses were largest in decision tasks, and review is a decision task.

The identity mechanics and the July 2026 mandate come from Microsoft's documents [vendor]. Check them against your own tenant. Personal-account figures are security-vendor telemetry [vendor]. No study compares agents with user accounts against agents called on demand at organisational scale. No rigorous study shows how notetakers change what people say.
